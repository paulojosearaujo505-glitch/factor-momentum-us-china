Robustness Checks

To ensure that the baseline results are not driven by a single arbitrary parameter choice, I conducted two robustness checks: the inclusion of transaction costs and an alternative lookback period.

In practice, monthly rebalancing incurs trading fees and bid-ask spread costs. To ensure the strategy is economically viable, I apply a conservative transaction cost of 10 basis points (0.10%) per trade to the 2021–2025 U.S. matched sample. The gross Sharpe ratio for this period was 0.84. After accounting for transaction costs, the net Sharpe ratio was 0.87. 
The slight increase in the risk-adjusted return is due to the strategy's relatively low turnover. The FMOM strategy does not trade every factor every month; it only trades when a factor's 12-month trailing return crosses zero (flipping from positive to negative or vice versa). Because the trading costs are only applied during these active months, they reduce the overall volatility of the portfolio slightly more than they reduce the returns. Ultimately, this confirms that the strategy remains highly robust and economically viable even after realistic trading frictions are deducted.

The baseline strategy utilizes a 12-month trailing return, following the standard momentum methodology of Jegadeesh and Titman (1993). To test the sensitivity of this choice, we re-run the strategy using a shorter 6-month lookback period. 

Under the 6-month lookback, the U.S. full-sample Sharpe ratio drops from 0.50 to 0.38. More dramatically, the Chinese sample Sharpe ratio drops from 0.18 to -0.11, turning the strategy into a losing proposition. This confirms that the 12-month formation period is optimal for this specific application. A 6-month lookback captures too much short-term market noise and mean-reversion, which erodes the underlying factor momentum premium. This result reinforces the robustness of the baseline 12-month specification and aligns with the established literature on the ideal momentum horizon.
