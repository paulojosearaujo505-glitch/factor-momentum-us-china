Literature Review

1. The Foundation: Stock Momentum and Its Limits
The foundation of this study rests on the momentum anomaly, one of the most robust and heavily documented empirical regularities in asset pricing. Jegadeesh and Titman (1993) demonstrated that a strategy of buying stocks that have performed well over the past three to twelve months and selling past losers generates significant positive returns. This finding fundamentally challenged the weak-form efficient market hypothesis and established that asset returns exhibit positive autocorrelation at the individual stock level. Recognizing that this phenomenon could not be explained by traditional risk-based models, Carhart (1997) extended the Fama-French three-factor model by introducing a momentum factor (MOM), constructed as the spread between past winners and losers. Since then, momentum has become a canonical factor in empirical asset pricing.

However, while stock momentum is pervasive in U.S. markets, the academic literature has long debated its underlying economic mechanism. Is it compensation for systematic risk, or does it arise from behavioural underreaction to information? This debate remained unresolved at the stock level until recent research shifted the analytical lens from individual stocks to the underlying factors themselves.

2. The Breakthrough: Factor Momentum
Building on the foundation of stock momentum, a recent breakthrough in the literature demonstrates that momentum is fundamentally a factor-level phenomenon. Ehsani and Linnainmaa (2022) show that the common factors used to explain stock returns—such as size, value, profitability, and investment—exhibit strong positive autocorrelation. Consequently, a strategy that goes long the factors with positive trailing returns and shorts the factors with negative trailing returns generates significant risk-adjusted returns. 

Crucially, Ehsani and Linnainmaa (2022) demonstrate that stock momentum is largely a byproduct of factor momentum. When the momentum factor itself is included in a factor momentum strategy, stock-level momentum becomes redundant. This finding aligns with Asness, Moskowitz, and Pedersen (2013), who document that momentum and value strategies exhibit correlated premia across a wide variety of asset classes globally. The implication is profound: momentum is not merely a quirk of individual stock trading, but rather a reflection of the persistent trending behavioural of systematic risk factors.

3. The China Puzzle and Construction Caveats
While the evidence for factor momentum in the U.S. is compelling, extending this analysis to the Chinese A-share market introduces a unique set of complications. First, standard stock momentum is famously weak or non-existent in China. Chui, Titman, and Wei (2010) provide a behavioural explanation for this cross-country variation, showing that momentum profits are positively associated with cultural individualism. In collectivist cultures like China, different patterns of overreaction and underreaction, driven by conformity, largely weaken the standard momentum anomaly.

Second, constructing standard asset pricing factors in China requires careful methodological adjustments. Liu, Stambaugh, and Yuan (2019) demonstrate that mechanically applying the U.S. Fama-French methodology to Chinese data yields biased results. They argue that the smallest 30% of Chinese firms act as "shell" companies for reverse mergers, distorting the size factor, and that the earnings-to-price (E/P) ratio dominates book-to-market (B/M) in capturing value effects. Any rigorous study of Chinese factors must account for these structural market differences.

Despite the weakness of stock momentum and these construction challenges, emerging literature suggests that factor momentum remains robust in China. Recent studies (e.g., Pacific-Basin Finance Journal, 2023; Finance Research Letters, 2024) construct factor momentum strategies in the A-share market and find strong, positive returns, with some strategies yielding annualized Sharpe ratios exceeding 1.0. These papers argue that factor momentum may represent the momentum anomaly in China, succeeding where individual stock momentum fails.

4. The Gap: A Like-for-Like Comparison
Despite these advances, a significant gap remains in the literature. Factor momentum has been rigorously documented in the United States (Ehsani & Linnainmaa, 2022) and, separately, in China (2023; 2024). However, these studies utilize different factor sets, varying lookback periods, and distinct construction methodologies. To date, no study has applied a unified, like-for-like specification to both markets over a comparable recent horizon to directly compare their performance.

This paper fills that gap. By applying an identical strategy, a 12-month lookback on a standardized five-factor set (Mkt-RF, SMB, HML, RMW, CMA), to both markets, this study provides a direct comparison of factor momentum dynamics. Due to data availability constraints, the Chinese sample spans the most recent five years (2021–2025). To ensure a rigorous, apples-to-apples comparison, the U.S. sample is matched to this exact same five-year window, while also evaluating the strategy over its full historical U.S. sample for broader context. This approach allows us to isolate whether factor momentum is a universal market dynamic, or whether its strength is contingent on market-specific characteristics like cultural behavioural biases and structural constraints. If factor momentum works in China where stock momentum fails, it provides strong evidence that factor-level trends capture a deeper, more universal economic reality than stock-level trends.
