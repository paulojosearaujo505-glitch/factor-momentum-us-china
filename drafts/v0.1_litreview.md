Literature review

The foundation of this study rests on the momentum strategy, one of the most used trading strategies. Jegadeesh and Titman (1993) demonstrated that a strategy of buying stocks that have performed well over the past three to twelve months and selling past losers generates significant positive returns. This finding fundamentally challenged the weak-form efficient market hypothesis and established that asset returns exhibit positive autocorrelation at the individual stock level. Recognizing that this phenomenon could not be explained by traditional risk-based models, Carhart (1997) extended the Fama-French three-factor model by introducing a momentum factor, constructed as the spread between past winners and losers. 

Building on the foundation of stock momentum, recent literature has shifted the analytical lens from individual stocks to the underlying factors themselves. Ehsani and Linnainmaa (2022) show that the common factors used to explain stock returns such as size, value, profitability, and investment exhibit strong positive autocorrelation. Consequently, a strategy that goes long the factors with positive trailing returns and shorts the factors with negative trailing returns generates significant risk-adjusted returns. Crucially, they demonstrate that stock momentum is largely a byproduct of this factor momentum. This finding aligns with Asness, Moskowitz, and Pedersen (2013), who document that momentum and value strategies exhibit correlated premia across a wide variety of asset classes globally. 

While the evidence for factor momentum in the U.S. is compelling, extending this analysis to the Chinese A-share market introduces a unique set of complications. First, standard stock momentum is famously weak. Chui, Titman, and Wei (2010) provide a behavioral explanation for this cross-country variation, showing that momentum profits are positively associated with cultural individualism. In collectivist cultures like China, different patterns of overreaction and underreaction, driven by conformity, largely weaken the standard momentum anomaly.

Second, constructing standard asset pricing factors in China requires careful methodological adjustments. Liu, Stambaugh, and Yuan (2019) demonstrate that mechanically applying the U.S. Fama-French methodology to Chinese data yields biased results. They argue that the smallest 30% of Chinese firms act as “shell” companies for reverse mergers, distorting the size factor, and that the earnings-to-price (E/P) ratio dominates book-to-market (B/M) in capturing value effects. However, the CSMAR dataset for Fama French 5 factors used in this study account for these structural differences by excluding the smallest firms.

Nevertheless, a significant gap remains in the literature. Factor momentum has been well documented in the U.S (Ehsani & Linnainmaa, 2022) and, separately, in China. However, these studies apply different factor sets, varying time periods, and distinct construction methodologies. To date, no study has applied a unified, apple to apple specification to both markets over a comparable recent time to directly compare their performance.

This paper fills that gap. By applying an identical strategy, a 12-month lookback on a standardized five-factor set (Mkt-RF, SMB, HML, RMW, CMA), to both markets, this study provides a direct comparison of factor momentum dynamics. Due to data availability constraints, the Chinese sample spans the most recent five years (2021–2025). To ensure apples-to-apples comparison, the U.S. sample is matched to this exact same five-year window, while also evaluating the strategy over its full historical U.S. sample. This two-market comparison allows us to test whether factor momentum is a broadly robust dynamic that transcends borders, or whether its efficacy is fundamentally constrained by the behavioral biases and structural frictions unique to specific markets
